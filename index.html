It all starts here

None of This Works Without the Right Superintendent.

The board's biggest decision in the next term is who leads this district. Here's the job: get St. Tammany back to number one. It's a big job, and it comes with a big paycheck: $284,775 a year in the 2026-27 budget. If you're not ready to get us back to number one, don't apply.

The next superintendent has to:

  1. Set the Goal Out Loud: back to number one in Louisiana, with a public plan and yearly checkpoints so everyone can see the progress.
  2. Win Families Back: treat every family that left for a charter, private school or home schooling as a family we can earn back, and find out why they left.
  3. Put Pay and Morale First: keep good teachers and staff, fill vacancies and make St. Tammany the place people want to work again.
  4. Protect Every Child, Openly: handle every abuse report immediately and transparently, because parents won't come back to a district they don't trust.
  5. Spend Every Dollar Like It's Ours: review the budget top to bottom and partner with every agency in the parish to save money.

These are ideas to explore, not decisions. Before anyone calls an idea crazy or says it will never happen, it deserves a fair look: first, can it actually be done, and second, is it worth doing? Getting answers means getting out of our comfort zone and past "this is how we've always done it." It will take input from many subject-matter experts and many sources, not one person's word.

The Short Answer

How much money can the district free up every year?

About $1 million to $5 million a year from spending trims alone. Adding staff reductions through retirements and resignations, paced to falling enrollment, brings that to roughly $4 million in 2027-28, growing to about $11 million a year by 2031-32. No one loses a job and no building changes use.

How much would meaningful permanent raises cost?

Each 1% raise for the roughly 5,190 non-management employees paid from the General Fund costs about $3.13 million a year. Staff paid from other funds, like school lunch and grants, add about $290,000. A 5% raise costs about $15.7 million a year. Every raise here is a percentage of each person's current pay, so every salary schedule moves in proportion.

What would make those raises sustainable?

Turning the district around. Students bring state money with them, so winning families back is the one source that grows the budget without cutting anything or raising anyone's taxes. Win back the students lost since 2019 and the $27 million gap waiting in 2027-28 closes on its own; keep growing with the parish and there is money left for raises. Add a top-to-bottom review of the budget, smart savings and partnerships with other parish agencies, and push the state to make its stipend a permanent raise.

The Bottom Line: this is a priorities problem, not a waste problem. Audits are clean and central administration is lean compared with peer districts. But local tax revenue grew strongly while teacher pay lost ground to inflation, and the district kept $38 million to $57 million more in reserve than its own 19% guideline every year from 2019 to 2025.

    What the Proposal Means for Your Paycheck

    The big idea

    Reach the top step in 18 years, not 36. Half the time.

    Today
    36 years to the top step*
    Proposed
    18 years
    top step, plus yearly cost-of-living raises
    0510152025303540
    Years working for the district (teacher example; support staff take 38 years today)

    Today's pay scale has 46 small levels. The proposal replaces them with 10 bigger steps, modeled on the federal government's General Schedule: the first three raises come one year apart, the next three two years apart, and the last three three years apart. The top step pays the same; you just get there twice as fast. After that, cost-of-living raises keep your pay growing instead of flattening out.

    *Only if every yearly step is funded. See "Read this first" below.

    Read This First

    • The 36 years is a best case. Today's scale gets a teacher to the top in 36 years only if the district funds a step raise every single year. It hasn't always: the salary schedule was frozen for four years, 2013-14 through 2016-17, and starting pay for teachers didn't move from 2010-11 through 2016-17. Every frozen year pushes the top step further away.
    • Stipends are not raises. One-time stipends, like the state's $2,000 for certified staff and $1,000 for support staff this year, are paid once and then they're gone. They don't change your pay scale, so next year's pay doesn't build on them.
    • What's Counted: base pay from the district's own 2026-27 salary schedules, plus the raise you pick. Not counted: stipends, supplements, extra-duty pay and the bus mileage allowance.
    • These are proposals, not promises. What the 10-step scale would cost can't be known until we see the payroll data we requested.

    Reaching the top of the scale doesn't mean no more raises. Reaching the top ends the yearly step, not raises. A percentage raise like this one, and any future cost-of-living adjustment (COLA), raises every level of the schedule, including the top. That's why the proposed scale doesn't flatten out after 18 years: open "See every year: graph and tables" above and use the COLA buttons to see pay keep rising. A teacher at the top of the bachelor's scale earns $74,033 today; with a 25% raise, that becomes $92,541, or $18,508 more every year. Louisiana teacher retirement is based on your highest-paid years, so a higher top step also means a bigger pension.

    What the 10-step scale costs is still unknown, and we won't guess. Moving up faster costs more than the raise alone, because employees in the middle of their careers reach higher pay sooner. How much more depends on how many employees sit at each level today, which is exactly what the payroll data we requested will show. The change would be phased in, and no one's pay would go down: each current employee would move to the step at or above their pay after the raise.

    Can a District Paycheck Cover the Basics?

    The pay schedules show what the district pays. Here is what that pay is worth in the real world: how it stacks up against what the same kinds of jobs pay around here, and against what it costs a family to get by.

    What These Numbers MeanTap to open
    Typical household income

    Line up every household from the lowest income to the highest. The one in the middle is the median, the “typical” household: half earn more, half earn less.

    A household is everyone living in one home, counted together: a single person, a retired couple, roommates or a family with children. Its income is everything everyone in the home brings in before taxes: every paycheck, plus Social Security, retirement and other income. So it isn’t one worker or one kind of family. The average St. Tammany household has 2.55 people; the Louisiana average is 2.42.

    Families raising children

    For families with children under 18, the typical income is $115,061 in St. Tammany and $76,480 statewide. Like household income, this counts every earner in the family together.

    All Census figures here are survey estimates for 2025, so each one has some room for error.

    The poverty line

    The federal government sets it every year. For 2026 it starts at $15,960 for one person and adds $5,680 for each additional person, so a family of four is $33,000.

    It’s the same everywhere in the lower 48 states and isn’t adjusted for local costs. Assistance programs use it as a yardstick, which is why the family table below measures each paycheck against it.

    What it takes to get by

    The United Way’s ALICE budget (Asset Limited, Income Constrained, Employed) adds up the bare-bones cost of living: housing, child care, food, transportation, health care, a basic cell phone plan and taxes, with nothing set aside for savings.

    For two adults with an infant and a preschooler in Louisiana, that comes to $81,912 a year, about two and a half times the poverty line. Families between the two lines earn too much to count as poor but still can’t cover the basics.

    Four in ten St. Tammany households (40%) earn less than it takes to cover basic costs, by the United Way’s ALICE measure. That count includes families below the poverty line and the working families just above it.

      The Basics

      The district's plan splits the $325 million four ways:

      Can bond money pay for raises?

      No. The district says so itself: "Bonds only fund facility projects and can't be used for salaries." Bond money sits in its own construction funds, and the budget book says those funds "cannot be used for operational expenses." So when someone asks, "They found $325 million for buildings, why not for teachers?", the honest answer is that this money can only go to buildings, equipment and buses.

      It's paid back from the 13.6 mills the school board levies for debt. In 2026-27 that tax brings in about $44 million, and the district pays $37.1 million in principal and interest on all of its bonds.

      Where buildings and paychecks do compete

      Bond money can't pay salaries, but the General Fund can, and the board has been moving General Fund money into construction. Over the six years from 2020-21 to 2025-26, it moved $54.6 million of operating money into the Parishwide Construction and Roofing funds. That's the same pot raises come from.

      Some of that paid for real needs, like roofs. Still, it was a choice the board made each year. In 2026-27 the budget moves nothing into construction and pulls $5 million back out of the Parishwide Construction Fund to help balance the General Fund. That shows the money can go either way. Now that a $325 million bond exists for buildings, operating money should go to people first.

District 7 has 4.6% of the parish's students and was promised 6.8% of the school-building money. That's a fair deal on paper. But a promise only counts when the work starts. Bonne Ecole, St. Tammany Junior High and Abita Springs already have contractors lined up. Lacombe doesn't have a bid date.

        "Chahta-Ima and Bayou Lacombe were promised $17 million eighteen months ago. Three other schools have contractors. When do our kids get out of the portables?"

        The district's consultant forecasts enrollment at Lakeshore and Monteleone dropping over the next ten years, and neither school is on the list. Local leaders question that forecast. If the board is planning around it, it should say so in public.

        Ben's Rule for the Bond

        If the district can spend it, it can account for it. Big bond, big scrutiny.

        1. Fix in the right order

          Safety first, then the condition of the building, then room for students, then the nice-to-haves. Everyday classrooms come before showcase projects.

        2. One page, every project, every month

          Each project gets its estimate, its contract amount, who's building it, its schedule and its status, posted online and updated monthly, like the district already does for its 2019 bond money.

        3. Publish the borrowing schedule

          Tell taxpayers when the remaining $275 million will be borrowed, and how each sale keeps the tax rate flat.

        4. Make the budget match the work

          If the board awards bond work, the adopted budget should show it. Taxpayers shouldn't have to piece it together from agendas.

        5. Explain every ranking

          Why one school went first and another waits, or isn't on the list at all, should be written down where parents can read it. That includes Monteleone and Lakeshore.

        6. Let the bond pay for buildings

          Before moving General Fund money into construction, show why the bond can't cover it. Operating money goes to people first.

        Making the Raise Real

        What raises cost, how we could pay for them, and what a full 25% raise would take, starting from the facts.

        The Facts We Start FromWhat Raises CostHow We Could Pay for ItWhat a 25% Raise Would Take

        The Facts We Start From

        • Reserves: the General Fund balance was $148.6 million at June 30, 2025, about 31% of spending, against a board guideline the district's audits call a 19% maximum.
        • Revenue: local property and sales taxes brought in $78.9 million more a year in FY2025 than in FY2019, while state funding fell.
        • Staffing: total positions grew from 5,447 in FY2016 to 6,100 in FY2025 (+12%) while enrollment fell 5%. How many positions coded as teachers actually teach a class is not in the public record.
        • Administration: central office spending per student is second-lowest of the peer districts compared.
        • Leader in Me: about 90% of the program's cost is federal grant money that cannot legally pay salaries.
        • Audits: clean opinions in every year reviewed. There is no evidence of fraud; the issue is priorities.

        Gap, scenario, phasing and savings figures are estimates built on the district's verified 2026-27 budget and 2019-2025 audited reports. All raises are figured as a percentage of pay. They are incomplete until the payroll data we requested from the district is released and reviewed; the district says that release is delayed while its chief financial officer is out on leave.

        Costs include the district's retirement contribution, Medicare and workers' compensation. Health insurance is paid per person and does not rise with a raise.

        Staff paid from other funds. The costs above cover employees paid from the General Fund. The lunch fund has almost no cushion, so in practice the General Fund would cover its share, and the grant share would come out of each grant's fixed award. See All the Money, Every Fund.

        How We Could Pay for It

        First: A Top-to-Bottom Review

        Not one more tax dollar until every possible saving inside the budget has been found. What the board controls:

        • Trim Lower-Priority Spending in the central office and the $10.2 million discretionary pool: about $1 million to $5 million a year.
        • Reduce Staff Through Retirements and Resignations at about the pace enrollment is falling. No one loses a job. Worth about $1.8 million in the first year, growing to about $9 million a year by 2031-32.
        • Look at an Early Retirement Incentive to speed up attrition. We don't yet know whether one would pay off or what form it should take. One tool is already in state law: the board may pay retiring employees for up to 45 days of unused sick leave instead of the standard 25, as long as the same policy applies to everyone. Any incentive must fit state retirement law and the constitution's ban on giving away public money, so it would need a legal opinion, a cost study and the payroll data we have requested. It saves money only when the position is not refilled, or is refilled lower on the salary schedule. Not counted in the figures on this page.
        • Put the Savings Ideas and Partnerships to Work, and grow enrollment. Both are laid out under Every Dollar.

        Phase the Raise, Lowest-Paid Employees First

        Recurring money arrives over time, as savings take hold and students come back. Giving support staff 5% first, at about $4.4 million a year, and everyone else as the money arrives keeps reserves above the board's 19% guideline the whole time. Everyone ends at the same 5% for the same permanent cost of about $15.7 million a year.

        Use Reserves as a Bridge, Never a Paycheck

        The reserve above the guideline is one-time money. It can start a raise a year early while recurring money arrives, but paying a permanent 5% raise from reserves alone would drain them to about 1% of spending within five years.

        Other Ways to Fund the Raise, Most to Least Desirable

        None of these are counted in the five-year plan, which conservatively assumes enrollment keeps falling, no buildings repurposed or divested, and no positions reduced beyond normal attrition.

        1. Grow Enrollment by Restoring the District's Rank and Reputation

          About $5,100 of state money follows each student. Winning back the 3,100 students lost since 2019 would bring about $16 million a year, roughly a 5% raise.

          The parish grew 6% from 2020 to 2025 while public school enrollment fell 8% from 2019 to 2025. St. Tammany ranked 1st in Louisiana in the early 2000s and 18th in 2024-25. Getting back to the top, and proving every child-abuse report is handled immediately and openly, is how families come back from charter, private and home schooling.

        2. Repurpose or Divest Under-Used Buildings

          Roughly $0.8 million to $1.4 million a year for each building divested. Repurposed space brings in rent or shares costs, depending on the use.

          Some buildings will always be schools, including historic campuses like Bayou Lacombe and Chahta-Ima. Where a school isn't using all of its space, put that space to work. It can be as simple as a satellite office: central office employees who live nearby could work from a district building close to home instead of driving to Covington every day, saving them gas and time and putting the space to use. Other options include early-childhood programs, adult education and job training, partner agencies, or community use that helps cover the cost of running the building. A building that no longer serves students could be leased or sold. Any change needs a capacity study and public hearings first.

        3. Reduce Positions Faster Than Normal Attrition

          About $8 million a year for every 100 positions.

          It pays some employees with other employees' jobs, raises class sizes and hurts recruiting. Retirements and resignations, paced to enrollment, reach much of the same savings without anyone losing a job.

        Far-Flung, Last-Resort Options. Taxes are not the plan. For the record, state law lets the board restore tax rates rolled back after the 2024 reassessment (about $11 million a year, about $77 on a $300,000 home), and voters can approve a dedicated pay tax. Those stay at the very bottom of the list, behind everything on this page.

        What a 25% Raise Would Take

        A 25% raise costs about $79 million a year, and if enrollment keeps falling the district must also close a gap of $36 million or more. The good news: growth closes the gap by itself.

        Partnering to Save

        Every public agency in the parish is funded by the same taxpayers. The school district, parish government and public works, the fire districts, the Sheriff's Office and the cities all buy fuel, maintain vehicles, insure buildings, buy technology and prepare for hurricanes, mostly on separate contracts. A parishwide cooperative purchasing group could lower costs for all of them.

        Where Buying Together Could Save

        • Fuel: diesel with public works and the fire districts, gasoline with the Sheriff and the cities.
        • Fleet: parts, tires and outside repair contracts.
        • Insurance: property, liability and possibly pooled health coverage.
        • Hurricane Readiness: generators, debris removal and emergency supplies.
        • Technology, Supplies and Shared Space.
        • School Security: review the resource officer contract with the Sheriff's Office to lower costs while keeping an officer in every school.

        Ground Rules

        • Each agency keeps its own money, employees and decisions. No mergers.
        • Each agency pays its own share.
        • Public bid law still applies, and restricted money stays restricted.
        • Louisiana's constitution and Local Services Law already allow it.

        Every agency in this parish spends the same taxpayers' money. It makes sense to buy together.

        Wish List: Help From the State

        Long-term ideas that would need the Legislature and the Governor's Office of Homeland Security and Emergency Preparedness (GOHSEP) to act. Not counted in any figure on this page.

        School buses and school buildings are part of Louisiana's disaster response. Buses can move residents out ahead of a storm: in 2019, the Lafayette and Avoyelles parish school systems sent buses to help evacuate residents before Tropical Storm Barry. Gyms and cafeterias can serve as shelters. Keeping them ready is paid for out of school budgets, and as far as we can find, the state makes no standing yearly payment to school districts for that readiness.

        • Fleet Readiness Funds. A yearly state payment to districts whose buses are on call for evacuations, to help cover maintenance, tires and keeping spare buses road-ready.
        • Shelter Readiness Funds. A yearly state payment for school facilities that could house evacuees, to keep electrical service, generators, restrooms, showers and kitchens up to standard.

        Every dollar the state covers for readiness is a dollar the district's own budget can put toward pay. This idea comes from Ben's 27 years in the Louisiana Air National Guard, including hurricane response from Katrina on.
